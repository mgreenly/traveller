# Session 2: System Hopping - The Long Road to Rech, 1105

The old spacer was back, this time at the Starfall Cantina on Mora Station, where the artificial sunset painted everything in shades of burnt orange. The war memorial outside cast long shadows through the viewports—names of the dead from the Fifth Frontier War etched in black stone that stretched three decks high. He'd found himself a corner table where he could watch both the entrance and the service corridors, old habits from when being paranoid meant staying alive. A group of young Navy ensigns on shore leave had gathered around, their dress uniforms still crisp, their eyes still bright with ambition that hadn't been ground down by the realities of deep space.

"So you want to hear what happened after Regina?" He swirled the ice in his Vargr bourbon, watching it catch the dying light. "The Stardust Drifter's crew, standing in that downport with empty pockets and a three-million-credit problem. This was before the war, remember—when you could still hop systems without worrying about Zhodani raiders or wondering if the next jump would drop you into a combat zone."

He leaned back, the chair's servos whining in protest.

"They had to wait two weeks on Regina for the right ship. Two weeks! Can you imagine? These days, you'd be on the next boat out before station security could blink. But back then, they had options, and they chose the Jump-2 hauler heading straight to Dinom. Smart move—skip Jenghe entirely, save time and credits. Of course, Jump-2 on a budget hauler meant one thing: cryoberths. Frozen like high-grade protein shipments, stacked in the cargo hold between containers of agricultural machinery and sealed crates marked with Makhidkarun logos.

"But here's what the legend doesn't tell you—those two weeks weren't idle time. The crew, they worked Regina's downport like professionals. Ash—everyone called him Ashton back then, before he earned his other names—he volunteered at a free clinic in the port's lower levels. Place was overwhelmed with refugees from the plague zones, desperate souls who'd fled quarantined worlds with nothing but the clothes on their backs. Ash worked eighteen-hour shifts, treating everything from radiation burns to exotic parasites. Made himself indispensable. By week two, the clinic's chief physician was trying to hire him permanently, offering connections to every medical supplier in the subsector.

"Meanwhile, Sydney and Aria had found work in the port's maintenance bays. You know how downports are—always something breaking, always some ship limping in with blown couplings or fried nav arrays. Sydney could rebuild a thruster assembly blindfolded, his hands moving with the kind of muscle memory that comes from years of keeping dying ships alive. Aria worked the electronics benches, her military training showing in every precise solder joint, every perfectly calibrated circuit. 

"Mina, though—she was something else. Set herself up in the artisan quarter with nothing but some polymer stock and a portable fabricator she'd borrowed from a sympathetic merchant. Started sculpting these incredible pieces—abstract forms that somehow captured the essence of jump space, that twisted non-reality that drives lesser minds to madness. Within three days, she had collectors bidding against each other. Sold one piece to a subsector administrator's wife for enough to cover their food for the entire journey.

"Each of them pulled in twelve hundred credits that first week. Not much by today's standards, but back then? Before the war inflation? That was real money. They pooled it all, started building their war chest one credit at a time. The second week, they kept at it, establishing routines, building contacts. Mike spent his days coordinating, managing their growing fund, negotiating with passage brokers, always pushing for better rates.

"Then came the jump to Dinom. Seven days in cryo, and this is where the story gets dark. Sydney's cryoberth malfunctioned somewhere between the third and fourth day. Catastrophic refrigeration failure—his core temperature started climbing while his metabolism was still suppressed. By the time the automated systems triggered an alert, he was already going into organ failure. They pulled him out blue-lipped and barely breathing, his heart stuttering like a broken chronometer.

"But Ash—remember, he'd just spent two weeks in that clinic, seeing every kind of medical emergency the frontier could throw at you. He worked on Sydney for three straight hours, right there on the cargo deck between the shipping containers, using a field medkit and pure determination. Cardiac stimulants measured in micrograms, manual chest compressions that cracked ribs, even a desperate jury-rigged defibrillator using power cells from Aria's toolkit. When Sydney finally gasped and his eyes fluttered open, Ash collapsed beside him, both of them lying there on that cold deck grating, surrounded by the crew who'd gone from strangers to family in the space of a heartbeat.

"Sydney was bedridden for four days after that. Couldn't walk without his legs giving out, couldn't speak above a whisper. They had to carry him between ships at Dinom, then again at Dinomn. But he never complained, never suggested turning back. That's when they stopped being five individuals and became a real crew—when they literally carried each other forward.

"The system hops blur together after that. Dinom to Dinomn, five days in steerage on a mining hauler that reeked of processed ore. Dinomn to Wypoc, another week crammed into a converted cargo pod with twelve other passengers and a failing air recycler. At Dinomn, they saw it—the Scrap Vulture, docked at the far end of the commercial pier. Brutal looking ship, all welded armor plates and oversized weapon mounts. Something about seeing that ship made them all uneasy, though none of them could say why.

"Wypoc to Rech was the worst leg. No legitimate carrier would make that run—Rech's reputation preceded it. They ended up on a smuggler's boat, paying triple rate for the privilege of not asking questions about the other cargo. Six days of running dark, transponder off, hoping they wouldn't get picked up by patrol boats or worse, pirates who hunted the space between Wypoc and Rech like sharks in dark water.

"When they finally made Rech, they were down to their last few hundred credits. But Mina had been thinking ahead. She'd been practicing with her fabricator during the long jumps, and within hours of landing, she'd set up shop at the Alibi—the one upscale establishment in that cesspool of a port. Started cranking out forged holo-IDs for anyone with the credits. Rech being what it was, business was brisk. Miners who needed clean identities for the labor contracts, smugglers who required diplomatic credentials, even a few folks who just wanted to disappear entirely.

"It took them a week of searching, but finally, there she was—Captain Kaelen, holding court at the Alibi's main bar. The *Void Gambit* crew had just come off a run to Vilis, flush with credits and looking for their next score. The negotiation was brutal. Kaelen wanted five million up front—no credit, no promises, just cold hard cash transferred before they lifted. 

"That's when they made the deal that would haunt them for months. Silas Kane—he'd set up shop on Rech by then, running his loan operation out of a converted warehouse that looked like a fortress. Ten million credits he gave them, at terms that would make a Vargr corsair blush. But they signed. What choice did they have? Time was bleeding away, and every hour they delayed was another hour lost.

"The funds transferred that night. Five million to Kaelen, the rest for supplies, bribes, and contingencies. By dawn, they were loading onto the *Void Gambit*, Sydney still weak but walking on his own, the rest of them checking weapons and supplies with the grim efficiency of people who knew they were heading into a fight."

The old spacer stood, dropping a credit chip on the table that clattered in the sudden silence.

"That was the journey—weeks of frozen sleep and bad air, of cramped quarters and mounting debt, of watching Sydney die and come back. But they made it to Rech, found their pilot, and secured their funding. The easy part was over."

He gave a bitter laugh as he headed for the door.

"Next time, I'll tell you about the Djinni run itself. About how they snuck past an Imperial Navy corvette, and what they found on that ghost ship. But that's a story that needs stronger drink than this place serves."

The ensigns watched him go, then looked at each other, wondering if they'd ever accumulate stories worth telling—and whether surviving long enough to tell them was worth the price.
