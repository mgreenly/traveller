# Prologue: The Storyteller

The old spacer had been haunting downport bars across the Spinward Marches for the better part of a decade now. Those who paid attention—and few did anymore—might notice he always ordered the same drink: Solomani whiskey when he could get it, Vargr bourbon when he couldn't, and rotgut synthetic when credits ran thin. He had that particular look of someone who'd spent too many years in recycled air and artificial gravity—skin like old leather, eyes that had seen jump space one too many times, and hands that trembled until the second drink steadied them.

Nobody knew his name. In some ports they called him "the Chronicler," in others simply "Old Timer." He never corrected them either way. What they did know was that for the price of a drink—sometimes two if the story ran long—he'd tell you about the old days. The days before the Fifth Frontier War turned half the sector into graveyards and the other half into armed camps. The days when a crew could still make their fortune in the spaces between the stars, if they had the nerve for it.

His stories always centered on the same crew: the legendary travelers who'd flown the *Stardust Drifter* through impossible odds. He called them heroes, though he was quick to add that heroes were just ordinary people who hadn't had the good sense to quit when they should have. He knew their names—Ash, Sydney, Aria, Mina—spoke them with a familiarity that suggested more than casual knowledge. But when pressed on how he knew such details, he'd just smile that crooked smile of his and order another round.

The stories changed depending on his audience and his sobriety. Sometimes the crew barely escaped with their lives from situations that, in other tellings, they'd handled with casual expertise. Details shifted like sand—was it three days or five they'd waited on Regina? Was the loan from Kane eight million or ten? But the heart of it, the spine of the legend, that remained constant: four strangers brought together by an inheritance that was equal parts fortune and curse, forging themselves into something greater through trials that would have broken lesser souls.

Those who'd heard multiple versions of his tales noticed something else: he never spoke about what happened after. After the war. After the crew became the names that every child in the Marches knew, that every merchant invoked for luck before a difficult jump. When someone once asked him directly what became of them, his eyes went distant and he muttered something about "some stories don't have endings, just places where you stop talking."

He moved from port to port with no apparent pattern, showing up in bars just as the evening crowd was settling in, disappearing before station security did their morning sweeps. Some said he was running from something. Others claimed he was searching for something. A few, those who noticed the way he sometimes touched an old scar on his left hand when he spoke of the crew's desperate moments, wondered if perhaps he wasn't running or searching at all—just remembering.

The war had been over for fifteen years now. The physical scars on planets and stations had mostly healed, though the psychological ones ran deeper. The old Imperial order had survived, battered but unbroken, and life in the Marches had found a new equilibrium. But in the quiet moments between drinks, when the bar noise faded to a distant hum, the old spacer would stare at nothing and whisper names like a prayer or a penance.

What was true and what was embellishment in his stories? Did it matter? The young traders and Navy ensigns who listened with wide eyes weren't there for historical accuracy. They came for the possibility that somewhere out there, beyond the next jump point, their own legend was waiting to be written. They came to believe that ordinary people could become extraordinary, that a crew of misfits could change the course of history, that adventure still existed in a universe that felt increasingly small and regulated.

And so he told his stories, night after night, port after port. Stories of a time before the war, when four strangers received mysterious letters and decided to chase an impossible dream. Stories that began in a law office on Regina and led through the dark spaces between stars. Stories that might have been true, or might have been the fevered imaginings of an old drunk, or might have been something in between—memories filtered through time and trauma until even the teller couldn't separate fact from fiction anymore.

But here's the thing about stories, especially the ones told in downport bars by old spacers with shaking hands and haunted eyes: sometimes the truth is less important than what we need to believe. And what the old spacer needed to believe—what he needed everyone to believe—was that once upon a time, before the war changed everything, there was a crew called the Star Duster's crew who did the impossible.

And maybe, just maybe, they really did.

The stories that follow are his stories, as he told them, as he remembered them, as he needed them to be. They begin on Regina, in the year 1105 of the Third Imperium, when the Fifth Frontier War was still just a gathering storm that nobody saw coming.

Listen carefully. Buy him another drink if his glass runs empty. And remember: every legend started as just another story told in just another bar by just another spacer who claimed to know the truth.
